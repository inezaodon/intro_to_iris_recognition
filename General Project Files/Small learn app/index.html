The paper that started the field

If you remember one paper from tonight, this is it.

John Daugman (Cambridge) published How iris recognition works in IEEE Transactions on Circuits and Systems for Video Technology, January 2004. The algorithms themselves go back to his 1993 PAMI paper and 1994 US patent 5,291,560. By 2004 they had been licensed into almost every deployed iris system: British Telecom, Sandia, Panasonic, LG, Oki, Iridian, and others. Independent tests kept reporting zero false matches across millions of comparisons.

This 2004 paper is the explainer. It is not a “new method” paper. It is Daugman walking you through why the method is so unusually safe to search at national scale, with 9.1 million iris-pair comparisons from Britain, the USA, Japan, and Korea as evidence.

In plain EnglishThink of this as the physics textbook for iris recognition. Notre Dame’s ArcIris still starts from the same geometry (find the rings, unwrap them). Only the later “encode and match” steps got replaced by a neural net.
Don’t mix this upAdam’s email said “Prof. Daugman.” Spelling is Daugman, not Dougman. He was a colleague of the Notre Dame group and famously skeptical of replacing IrisCodes with deep learning at Aadhaar-like scale.

The thesis, in one sentence

Recognition is the unique failure of a test of statistical independence on iris phase.

Two different eyes, encoded into bits, look like two unrelated random bit strings. If you XOR them, about half the bits disagree — Hamming distance ≈ 0.50 — exactly like comparing tosses of a fair coin. That is the independence test passing: these are different people.

When you compare two images of the same eye, the bit strings agree far more than chance. Hamming distance drops well below 0.5, typically under 0.32. Independence has failed. That failure is the match.

This framing is the whole paper. Everything else — Gabor wavelets, rubber sheets, binomial histograms — exists to make that test fast, stable, and statistically airtight.

In plain EnglishYou are not “measuring how similar two pictures look.” You are asking: “Could these two bit strings have come from unrelated random sources?” If no, it is the same eye. If yes, it is someone else.

Why faces are a hard biometric, and irises are not

Pattern recognition only works when same-class variation is smaller than between-class variation.

A face is a social, 3-D, lighting-sensitive organ. Expression, pose, age, glasses, and illumination all change the same person’s image. Daugman cites early-2000s face tests where images taken a year apart still had 43–50% error. Meanwhile different faces share the same parts in the same layout, so between-person variation is limited. Intra-class is huge; inter-class is small. That is the wrong way around.

The iris is the opposite. It is an internal organ that happens to be visible. It is roughly planar, so lighting angle mostly scales brightness instead of reshaping features. Viewing-angle change is mostly an affine warp. Pupil dilation distorts the texture, but in a way you can invert with a rubber-sheet model. The pattern is insanely different from person to person.

In plain EnglishFaces of the same person can look more different than faces of two different people. Two irises of different people almost never look “accidentally similar” once you encode them as phase bits. That is why iris can do 1-to-many search and face, in 2004, mostly could not.

Biology: what you are actually looking at

The visible iris is a random morphogenetic fingerprint, not a DNA barcode.

The iris starts forming in the third month of gestation and is mostly structurally done by the eighth month. Pigment can keep filling in after birth, which is why a baby’s eye color can change, but the mesh of the pattern is already there.

Texture parts you should be able to name: arching ligaments, furrows, ridges, Fuchs’ crypts (the pits Notre Dame’s CRYPTS method later uses), rings, corona, freckles, and the zigzag collarette. Color comes from melanin in the anterior layer and stroma. Blue eyes are mostly “no pigment,” so long wavelengths punch through and shorter ones scatter.

Under visible light you mostly see the trabecular meshwork. Under near infrared (NIR, 700–900 nm) you see deeper stromal structure. Dark brown irises that look featureless in a phone photo light up in NIR because melanin absorbs visible light but less NIR. That is why every serious iris camera is NIR, not RGB.

Don’t mix this upIridology — the claim that iris marks diagnose organ disease or personality — is cited in this paper as medical fraud (Berggren 1985; Simon et al. 1979). The texture is random, not a health map. Daugman is very clear about this.

Epigenetic, not genetic

Your left eye is as different from your right as from a stranger. Identical twins do not match.

Eye color is genetic. The detailed phase pattern is not. Daugman compared 648 left/right pairs from 324 people. Mean Hamming distance was 0.497 — statistically the same as comparing unrelated people (0.499). A small set of actual identical-twin pairs also sat at HD ≈ 0.507.

So the pattern is epigenetic: it comes from random events while the tissue is folding in the womb, not from a gene that says “put a crypt here.” That is fantastic for biometrics. You cannot steal someone’s iris code by knowing their family. You also cannot match left to right and call it the same identity.

In plain EnglishIf a system ever matches someone’s left eye to their right eye, that is a bug, not a feature. Notre Dame even stores an eye-side byte in the IREX template so left is never compared to right during 1:N search.
Number to keepUnrelated eyes: HD mean 0.499, σ = 0.0317. Genetically identical (L vs R): HD mean 0.497, σ = 0.031. Same distribution.

Capture: you need enough pixels, in NIR

If the iris is a tiny blurry disc, no algorithm downstream can invent texture that was never imaged.

Daugman wants at least 70 pixels of iris radius. Field systems in the paper typically got 100–140. Cameras were 640×480 monochrome CCD with NIR illumination, invisible to the subject so the pupil does not bounce around from a visible flash.

Some platforms used a wide-angle camera to find the face, then steered a narrow-angle pan/tilt camera onto the eye. Many just used a mirror or live video so the person put their own eye in the box.

Focus is measured in real time by high-frequency power in the 2-D Fourier spectrum of the frame. More sharp detail → more high-frequency energy. Either the lens hunts, or the machine beeps at you to lean in.

In plain EnglishThis is why “iris from a random selfie” is a different research problem. ISO later asked for on the order of 120 pixels across the iris diameter. Notre Dame’s IREX code still assumes 640×480-class images and even pads to 4:3 before the networks run.

Finding the iris: a circular edge detector

Search over center and radius until a circle sits on the strongest radial jump in brightness.

The pupil is a dark disc; the iris is a textured ring; the sclera is brighter. Daugman’s operator walks around a candidate circle, adds up image values on that circle, then asks: if I grow the radius a tiny bit, how fast does that sum change? At the true pupil or limbus (outer iris) boundary, that derivative explodes. Blur the search with a Gaussian so noise does not win. Repeat coarse-to-fine until you have single-pixel (x₀, y₀, r) for both rings.

In symbols, maximize over (r, x₀, y₀) the blurred radial derivative of a contour integral around the circle. You do not need to memorize the integral. You need to know it is a circular integro-differential edge detector.

The pupil search is constrained by the iris search, but they are not concentric. The pupil is often nasal and a bit down. Pupil radius can be 0.1 to 0.8 of iris radius. All three pupil parameters are estimated separately.

In plain EnglishImagine sliding a circle around the photo and inflating it until it “catches” the pupil rim, then doing it again for the brown-to-white rim. Eyelids are the same idea, except the path is an arc, not a full circle, then a spline is fitted.

The rubber-sheet model

Turn the ring into a rectangle whose height is always “pupil → limbus = 0 to 1.”

Even on-axis, the iris changes size with camera distance and with pupil dilation. Dilation is not a simple zoom: the inner hole grows and the tissue stretches. Daugman models this as a homogeneous rubber annulus, anchored at the outer rim, pulled by an off-center inner ring.

Every point on the iris gets coordinates (r, θ). Angle θ is 0 to 2π. Radius r is not pixels — it is a unit interval [0, 1] from the pupillary boundary to the limbus. Mix the two boundary curves:

x(r,θ) = (1−r) x_pupil(θ) + r x_limbus(θ)
y(r,θ) = (1−r) y_pupil(θ) + r y_limbus(θ)

Because the two centers can differ, this is a non-concentric polar map. ArcIris still does exactly this unwrap, to 512×64 pixels.

Rubber sheet: ring becomes a dimensionless rectangle. Pupil size drops out.

Don’t mix this upThis fixes size, translation, and pupil dilation. It does not fix head tilt / eye torsion. Rotation is handled later by sliding the IrisCode, not by rotating the photo.

Encoding: Gabor phase, not brightness

Ask, at many patches and scales: which quadrant of the complex plane is this texture in?

A 2-D Gabor wavelet is a little oriented ripple (a sine/cosine) under a Gaussian blob. Daugman had used these since the 1980s as a model of visual cortex. You drop one on a patch of iris, integrate, and get a complex number — a phasor with a real part and an imaginary part.

Then you throw away the length of that phasor (amplitude) and keep only the quadrant of its angle. Four quadrants → 2 bits. Gray/Grey code: moving to a neighboring quadrant flips only one bit, so small angle noise does not flip both bits at once.

Repeat across the iris at many sizes (0.15 mm to 1.2 mm on the real iris, an 8× range) and frequencies (3 octaves). Total: 2,048 phase bits (256 bytes). A second 2,048-bit mask marks bits that sat on eyelashes, eyelids, specularities, contact-lens edges, or junk SNR so matching can ignore them.

Why dump amplitude?Brightness tracks lighting, camera gain, and contrast. Phase tracks the layout of the texture — the zero-crossings of those wavelets. Two photos of the same eye under different lamps should still share phase. That is the whole trick.

The four quadrants, drawn

Each local patch votes for one of four 2-bit labels: 00, 10, 11, 01 around the plane.

Figure 2 in the paper: phase-quadrant demodulation. The gold dot is one patch’s phasor.

The sign of the real part is bit 1. The sign of the imaginary part is bit 2. That is the sgn operator in Daugman’s equation (2). You never store the actual angle, only which quadrant it landed in.

Even a badly out-of-focus eye still gets a code — the bits get set by CCD noise. That sounds like a bug. It is a feature: two different blurry irises do not collapse toward the same code, the way two blurry faces start to look like the same oval. Random noise vs random noise still XOR’s to HD ≈ 0.5.

In plain EnglishYou are turning “this patch of iris texture” into a 2-bit compass heading. Do that a thousand times. The heading sequence is the IrisCode.

Matching: fractional Hamming distance

Disagreeing bits, counting only bits both masks say are real iris.

HD = || (codeA XOR codeB) AND maskA AND maskB || / || maskA AND maskB ||

XOR is 1 where the two codes differ. AND with both masks throws away lashes, lids, glare. The denominator is “how many bits actually counted,” so HD is a fraction between 0 and 1. Perfect match would be 0. Unrelated eyes sit at 0.5.

This is not a neural network. It is Boolean logic that a CPU already has as a single instruction on 32-bit chunks. On a 300 MHz Sun workstation in 2004, Daugman quotes ~100,000 iris comparisons per second, and 10 µs to XOR two IrisCodes. That speed is why Aadhaar-scale search was imaginable with this representation and still painful with float embeddings.

Tiny exampleCodes 1101 and 1001, both fully unmasked. XOR = 0100 → one bit in four disagrees → HD = 0.25. If the second bit of either mask was 0 (occlusion), that bit is skipped and HD = 0/3 = 0.
Don’t mix this upLower HD = more similar. It is a dissimilarity. Cosine similarity in ArcIris is the opposite direction (higher = closer). Always check whether a matcher outputs distance or similarity before you pick a threshold.

The impostor histogram: 249 coin tosses

Different eyes disagree like a binomial with N ≈ 249, p = 0.5 — not 2,048 independent bits.

9,060,003 comparisons of different irises. Mean HD = 0.499, σ = 0.0317. If every one of the 2,048 bits were an independent coin, σ would be about 0.011 and the histogram would be a spike. It is wider, so bits are correlated (especially radially: nearby rings of the iris are not independent).

For a binomial, N = p(1−p)/σ². Plug in: N ≈ 249 degrees of freedom. The histogram is “what you get if you flip a fair coin 249 times and plot the fraction of heads.” A quantile-quantile plot of the left tail against that binomial is a straight line over three orders of magnitude. The model is not decorative. It is the false-match calculator.

Smallest impostor HD in that 9.1 million: 0.334. Chance of HD < 0.333 ≈ 1 in 16 million. Chance of HD ≤ 0.300 ≈ 1 in 10 billion.

In plain EnglishYou published 2,048 bits, but only ~249 of them are truly independent facts about the iris. That is still an enormous password. Discrimination entropy works out to about 3.2 bits per mm² of iris.

Rotation: do not rotate the image

Encode once, then circularly shift the bit code and keep the best HD.

Head tilt, cyclotorsion of the eye in the socket, and pan/tilt mirrors all rotate the iris in the image plane. Rotating the photograph with an Euler matrix is the expensive way. The cheap way: compute the IrisCode in one canonical orientation, then slide it in θ (the angular axis of the rectangle) and recompute HD. Daugman used 7 relative orientations and kept the minimum HD.

“Best of 7” is an extreme-value sample, so the impostor histogram shifts left: mean falls from 0.500 to 0.458, σ to 0.0196, smallest observed HD still about 0.33. He writes down the exact formula: if F₀(x) is the chance of a false match in one orientation, then after n tries it is 1 − [1 − F₀(x)]ⁿ. The theory matches the histogram.

In plain EnglishYou give the matcher a few “maybe the head was tilted” chances. That makes impostors look slightly more similar on average, which you must budget for in the threshold. You do not get a free lunch. Notre Dame’s HDBIF still does shifts (even shifts first, then ±1 around the winner, to hit IREX’s 25-second search cap).

The threshold table you should be able to recite

HD ≤ 0.32 is the folklore cutoff. Tighter if the gallery is huge.

These odds already include the 7-orientation “best of n” correction. In the non-ideal 7,070 genuine pairs vs 9.1 million impostors, max genuine HD was 0.327 and min impostor HD was 0.329 — a hair’s-breadth perfect separation on that particular dataset, at theoretical FMR ≈ 1 in 4 million.

Don’t treat 0.32 as magicGenuine scores move around with blur, motion, and cameras. Impostor scores do not — they are combinatorics. So you adapt the threshold to keep search-wide false matches rare, not to chase a pretty genuine mean.

Verification is easy. Identification is not.

A 99.9% verifier is useless as a search engine once N is a few thousand.

Verification (1:1): someone claims an identity, you check one template. Identification (1:N): you search everyone. If P₁ is the false-match chance of one comparison, the chance of at least one false match in a gallery of N is

P_N = 1 − (1 − P₁)^N ≈ N · P₁    when P₁ is small.

Daugman’s horror story: a face algorithm with P₁ = 0.001 (only 0.1% false matches, better than confusing all identical twins). At N = 200, P_N is already 18%. At N = 2,000, P_N is 86%. You will almost surely accuse somebody.

So an identifier must be roughly N times better than a verifier. Iris gets there because the binomial tail dies so fast that you can drop HD from 0.33 to 0.27 and absorb a million extra impostor chances. He targets P_N < 10⁻⁶ regardless of N by making the HD threshold adaptive.

Why Adam caresNIST IREX is identification, not verification. Ranking is FNIR at FPIR = 0.01 on 500,000 identities. That is Daugman’s 1:N problem with modern names. ArcIris is being judged on that problem, not on a lab EER.

The decision environment and d-prime

Plot genuine HDs on the left, impostor HDs on the right. Overlap is error. Separation is d′.

Two histograms: same-eye comparisons vs different-eye comparisons. If they barely overlap, you can pick a threshold. Daugman calls this the decision environment.

Non-ideal (different cameras, distances): genuine mean HD = 0.110, σ = 0.065; impostor mean 0.458, σ = 0.020; d′ = 7.3 from 2.3 million comparisons. Ideal lab (same camera, zoom, distance): genuine mean 0.019, and more than half of pairs hit HD = 0.00; d′ = 14.1.

d′ = |μ₁ − μ₂| / sqrt((σ₁² + σ₂²)/2). It does not depend on where you put the threshold. It tells you how much any FMR win will cost you in FNMR.

The deep factThe impostor distribution barely moves when imaging gets worse — bits of different eyes are still random. The genuine distribution slides right toward 0.5 as blur and noise eat the real texture. Quality does not make strangers look like you. Quality makes you look less like yourself.
d′ warningThis assumes roughly Gaussian scores. Iris papers quote it constantly, including the Notre Dame ArcIris paper. It is a convenience index, not a full ROC.

Speed, as of 2004 hardware

A third of a second to make a template. Ten microseconds to compare two of them.

OperationTime on a 300 MHz Sun

Localize eye and iris90 ms

Detect and fit both eyelids93 ms
Remove lashes and contact-lens edges78 ms
Demodulation / IrisCode creation102 ms
XOR comparison of two IrisCodes10 µs

Template creation is milliseconds because of image processing. Search is microseconds because of XOR. Daugman’s scaling advice: shard a national gallery into chunks of ~100,000 people, search them in parallel, and tighten each shard’s HD threshold so the union still has P_N < 10⁻⁶. He claims a national identification decision in about one second on banks of cheap CPUs.

Bridge to 2026IREX still cares about this split. Extract cap: 1.5 s. Search cap: 25 s against 500k templates. ArcIris spends its time in a ResNet, then matches with a cheap vector distance. CRYPTS spends its time in Earth Mover’s Distance and misses the search cap. Daugman’s speed argument never went away.

What the paper does not claim

Stable enough to recognize, not frozen like a wax statue, not a medical oracle.

He needs the pattern to be stable enough over years that genuine HD stays on the left of the decision environment. He does not need every crypt to be eternal. The test still works if up to about a third of the bits are corrupted — that is what HD ≤ 0.32 already allows.

He explicitly dumps iridology. He also notes that disease and aging can change an iris (Notre Dame later built whole datasets on that: Warsaw post-mortem, disease-iris). The 2004 paper’s operational claim is about healthy, living, NIR, cooperative capture.

He is not arguing neural nets can never encode irises. He is arguing that whatever you use must (1) produce something you can search at 1:N without false matches exploding, and (2) not confuse blurry samples with each other. That is the steel-man of “Daugman wasn’t a fan of deep learning.”

The FTE plot twist

Commercial VeriEye is #1 on IREX and looks nearly perfect on clean academic sets. It also refuses a lot of hard images (FTE 22.8% on VII-Q-R2, 7.7% on Q-FIRE). Worldcoin open-iris dies when quality drops (FTE 86% post-mortem). ArcIris almost never refuses, so when ISO-style scoring treats failure-to-enroll as a non-match, the open-source nets look much closer to commercial — and more usable for forensics.
